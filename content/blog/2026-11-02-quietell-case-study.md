# QuieTell: what designing for cat owners taught me about how people decide

Cats are very good at hiding when something is wrong.

They eat a little less. Sleep a little more. Sit by the window instead of on your lap. Each change is small enough to ignore, and by the time they add up to something you can't ignore, it can be late.

QuieTell started as an idea about that gap: the space between noticing something and doing something about it.

## It took longer than I planned

I'll be honest about this part. QuieTell started as an idea, moved into research, and then life got in the way. There were gaps. Weeks where the Figma file didn't move.

I'm glad I came back to it, because the long version taught me more than a quick one would have.

## What the research was really about

I started by looking at the market and at the people I was designing for: cat owners who love their cats and still miss the early signs.

The more I learned, the less this felt like a pet app and the more it felt like a study of how people make decisions. What makes someone act. What makes them wait. What they do right before they finally pick up the phone and call the vet.

A few things kept coming up.

**People don't trust their own memory.** "When did this start?" is one of the hardest questions to answer under stress. So QuieTell is built around short check-ins, and every check-in is kept on record. The goal is simple: show your vet exactly what shifted and when.

**Every cat has a different normal.** A quiet cat isn't a sick cat. So the app spends the first 7 days learning a baseline for each cat before it starts comparing. Changes are measured against her own normal, not against an average cat.

**People ask other people before they act.** Before most owners call a vet, they want to know if anyone else has seen the same thing. That's why there's a community, and why the App Store screenshot for it says "You're not the only one wondering."

**When the options don't fit, people go quiet.** A check-in list can't cover everything a cat might do. So I added an assistant for those moments. It already knows the cat, her normal and her check-ins. You describe what's going on in your own words and it helps you get clear.

**Trust comes from honesty about limits.** The assistant says "I'm not a vet" before the first message. It explains the cat's record and how the app works, and sends anything that needs a health opinion to the vet.

## The App Store screenshots

Most App Store screenshots list features. Nobody downloads a feature.

So every QuieTell screenshot leads with a worry a cat owner already has, in their words:

- "You're not the only one wondering"
- "Every check-in, on record"
- "Find your cat people"

The screen inside each frame is only there to prove the line above it.

## What it changed in me

This project made my judgment sharper. Not my Figma skills. My sense of what a person will actually do next, and what gets in the way.

Most of the design decisions in QuieTell aren't visual. They're about timing, memory, worry and trust. That's the part of product design I want to keep doing.
